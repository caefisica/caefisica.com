Integral representation of the $\delta$-function

there is a way to represent the $\delta$ function which is  
really, really really useful in practice:

$$\begin{aligned}
\boxed{\delta(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{ikx}\,dk}
\end{aligned}\tag{40}$$

REMEMBER ME!

this formula is worth remembering. It could save your life  
one day.

Before we prove it, let us try to understand it. if $x=0$ we

eg

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}1\,dk=\infty$$

which agrees with (35). When $x\ne0$ the integral can be split into  
sines and cosines. These oscillate indefinitely, which leads to  
destructive interference. Hence when $x\ne0$ we get 0.

To prove Eq (38) consider first the following integral ($x>0$)

$$\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{ikx-\gamma|k|}\,dk
&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{(ix-\gamma)k}\,dk
+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{(ix+\gamma)k}\,dk\\
&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left.\frac{e^{(ix-\gamma)k}}{ix-\gamma}\right|_{0}^{\infty}
+\frac{1}{2\pi}\left.\frac{e^{(ix+\gamma)k}}{ix+\gamma}\right|_{-\infty}^{0}\\
&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left[\frac{1}{\gamma-ix}+\frac{1}{\gamma+ix}\right]\\
&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left[\frac{\gamma+ix+\gamma-ix}{\gamma^2+x^2}\right]\\
&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma^2+x^2}
\end{aligned}$$
