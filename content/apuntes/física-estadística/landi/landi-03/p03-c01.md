Finding the moments $\langle x^m\rangle$ from the CF

the CF is the average of $e^{isx}$. Expanding the exponential  
in a Taylor series we get

$$\begin{aligned}
e^{isx}=1+isx+\frac{(is)^2}{2!}x^2+\frac{(is)^3}{3!}x^3+\cdots
\end{aligned}\tag{3}$$

Now we take the average and use the fact that $\langle\rangle$ is a linear  
operation:

$$\begin{aligned}
G(s)=\langle e^{isx}\rangle=1+is\langle x\rangle+\frac{(is)^2}{2!}\langle x^2\rangle+\cdots
\end{aligned}\tag{4}$$

we see from here results that if we know $G(s)$, we may expand  
it in a Taylor series and the moments will appear as the  
coefficients of the expansion.

More precisely, the Taylor series expansion of $G(s)$ is

$$\begin{aligned}
G(s)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{s^m}{m!}G^{(m)}(0)
\end{aligned}\tag{5}$$

where $G^{(m)}=\frac{d^mG}{ds^m}$, is the m-th derivative of $G$ with respect to $s$,  
evaluated at zero. we may write (4) as

$$\begin{aligned}
G(s)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(is)^m}{m!}\langle x^m\rangle
\end{aligned}\tag{6}$$

Thus we conclude that

$$\begin{aligned}
\langle x^m\rangle=\frac{G^{(m)}(0)}{i^m}
\end{aligned}\tag{7}$$

why this is useful: in principle to compute each $\langle x^m\rangle$ you need  
to do one integral. Using this method you compute just a  
single integral [$G(s)$] and then find the $\langle x^m\rangle$ by differentiation.
