We begin by introducing the function

$$
V(\sigma_1,\sigma_2)=\exp\left\{\beta J\sigma_1\sigma_2+\frac{\beta h}{2}(\sigma_1+\sigma_2)\right\}
\tag{3.12}
$$

This is almost a typical term in Eq. (3.11), except that I slightly altered the magnetic field term to make things look more symmetric. The partition function (3.11) may now be written as (please take your time to check that this is indeed correct)

$$
Z=\sum_{\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_N}V(\sigma_1,\sigma_2)V(\sigma_2,\sigma_3)\cdots V(\sigma_{N-1},\sigma_N)V(\sigma_N,\sigma_1)
\tag{3.13}
$$

The key to the **transfer matrix technique** is to recognize the similarity between this expression and the trace of a matrix.

Given some matrix $A$, its trace is the sum of the diagonal entries:

$$
\operatorname{tr}(A)=\sum_i A_{i,i}
$$

The trace of $A^2$, on the other hand, is

$$
\operatorname{tr}(A^2)=\sum_i(A^2)_{i,i}=\sum_{i,j}A_{i,j}A_{j,i}
$$

And the trace of $A^3$ is

$$
\operatorname{tr}(A^3)=\sum_{i,j,k}A_{i,j}A_{j,k}A_{k,i}
$$

Note how this is exactly what appears in Eq. (3.13), provided we interpret $V(\sigma_i,\sigma_j)$ as the matrix elements of a matrix $V$. This is acceptable since each $\sigma_i$ can take on only two values so that $V$ will be $2\times2$.

The matrix elements of $V$ can be computed directly from the definition in Eq. (3.12):$^3$

$$
V=
\begin{pmatrix}
V(1,1)&V(1,-1)\\
V(-1,1)&V(-1,-1)
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
e^{\beta(J+h)}&e^{-\beta J}\\
e^{-\beta J}&e^{\beta(J-h)}
\end{pmatrix}
\tag{3.14}
$$

The partition function in Eq. (3.13) may now be written as

$$
\boxed{Z=\operatorname{tr}(V^N)}
\tag{3.15}
$$

This is the idea of the transfer matrix: the matrix $V$ transfers the solution from one site to the other and the partition function is simply the trace of a product of $V$s.
