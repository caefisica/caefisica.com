This is so common that most people associate the word  
"paramagnetism" with "magnetization is linear in B".

To observe any deviation from a straight line, we need to  
go to very low temperatures. For instance, if $T=1$ K, you  
will already see an effect with fields of the order of $\frac{1}{2}T$.

The magnetization that is actually measured experimentally  
in $\mu_B\langle\sigma\rangle$. If we have $N$ independent spins, than the total  
magnetization will be

$$\begin{aligned}
M &= N\mu_B\langle\sigma\rangle \simeq \frac{N\mu_B^2B}{\mu_BT}
\end{aligned}\tag{47}$$

the susceptibility is defined as

$$\begin{aligned}
\chi &= \frac{\partial M}{\partial B}
\end{aligned}\tag{48}$$

Thus we see that

$$\begin{aligned}
\chi &= \frac{N\mu_B^2}{\mu_BT} = \frac{C}{T}
\end{aligned}\tag{49}$$

the constant $C=N\mu_B^2/\mu_B$ is called the Curie constant (it is  
not the specific heat!)
