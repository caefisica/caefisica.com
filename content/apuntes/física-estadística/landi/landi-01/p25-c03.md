The variance satisfies

$$\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(cx) &= c^2\operatorname{Var}(x)\\
\operatorname{Var}(x+c) &= \operatorname{Var}(x)
\end{aligned}\tag{3.6}$$

The first is reassuring: even if $c<0$, you always get a non-negative variance. The second shows that $\operatorname{Var}(x)$ is indeed a good measure of the spread of the distributions: if you shift it by $c$, the variance remains unaltered. I leave it to you to verify (3.6).

If $x$ has units of meter, then $\operatorname{Var}(x)$ will have units of $m^2$. It is therefore useful to have a definition of width in meters. For this reason we define the standard deviation

$$\begin{aligned}
SD(x) &= \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(x)}
\end{aligned}\tag{3.7}$$
